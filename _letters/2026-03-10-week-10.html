Dear Friends and Colleagues,

One of the most dangerous assumptions in this cycle is that advanced intelligence capacity can remain a premium service and still produce fair public outcomes. It cannot. If only the best-resourced institutions can afford accountable systems, then accountability itself becomes a luxury feature.

We see this pattern repeatedly. Smaller cities, nonprofits, and community-serving teams are expected to modernize rapidly, but are offered tools that are either prohibitively expensive, insufficiently transparent, or both. The result is a two-tier operating reality: one for institutions with procurement leverage and another for everyone else. That is not innovation. It is structural exclusion wearing modern language.

The corrective is straightforward, even if execution is hard. We need intelligence infrastructure that is affordable to adopt, auditable in operation, and adaptable to local context. Open architecture matters here because it lowers lock-in risk, improves institutional learning curves, and allows capability to spread without forcing dependence on a single technical gatekeeper.

This is not an anti-technology stance. It is a pro-governance stance. The question is not whether modern systems can produce useful outputs. The question is whether those systems can be trusted, inspected, and corrected in institutions that operate under public obligations.

If we believe civic systems should serve all residents, we cannot accept intelligence infrastructure designed only for those who can pay premium access costs. The public-interest standard requires broader distribution of capability, not deeper concentration of it.

That is the lane we are committed to building in.

With resolve,
Nicholas A. Cerbone
